Mortgage-Backed Securities Tumble as Fed Signals Extended Rate Plateau, Prepayment Risks Mount

Mortgage-backed securities suffered their steepest weekly decline in eight months as investors recalibrate portfolios amid persistent inflation data and Federal Reserve commentary signaling rates will remain elevated longer than anticipated. Agency MBS spreads widened sharply while prepayment speeds accelerated, eroding expected returns and triggering redemptions across major fixed-income funds focused on housing debt.

Rebecca Morrison and Thomas Caldwell in New York 14/03/2024 16:42:18

Mortgage-backed securities markets are experiencing significant turbulence as a confluence of adverse factors—including stubborn inflation metrics, rising Treasury yields, and deteriorating prepayment dynamics—converge to pressure valuations across the sector. The Bloomberg U.S. MBS Index declined 1.8% this week, marking the largest weekly loss since July 2023, while option-adjusted spreads on agency MBS widened by 12 basis points to 148 basis points over comparable Treasuries. Trading desks report heightened volatility as institutional investors pare exposure to housing-related debt amid mounting concerns about portfolio duration risk and negative convexity.

Federal Reserve officials' recent commentary has materially altered the rate trajectory expected by MBS investors, with markets now pricing in just one 25-basis-point cut before year-end compared to four cuts anticipated at the start of the quarter. This reassessment has particularly impacted higher-coupon MBS securities, where prepayment acceleration threatens to return principal to investors at inopportune times. Fannie Mae 6.0% and 6.5% coupon pools have underperformed significantly, declining 3.2% and 3.7% respectively over the past two weeks as refinancing activity picks up despite elevated absolute rate levels. Prepayment speeds measured by the Constant Prepayment Rate (CPR) jumped to 18.2% in February from 14.1% in January, well above the 12% level that most portfolio managers had modeled.

The technical backdrop for MBS has deteriorated markedly as the Federal Reserve continues its quantitative tightening program, allowing approximately $35 billion in agency MBS to roll off its balance sheet monthly without reinvestment. This persistent selling pressure comes as commercial banks—traditionally the largest buyers of agency MBS beyond the Fed—have significantly reduced their appetite for mortgage debt. Bank holdings of MBS declined by $127 billion over the past twelve months according to Federal Reserve data, as institutions prioritize shorter-duration assets and manage regulatory capital requirements more conservatively. Asset managers at Wellington Capital Partners note that traditional price-insensitive buyers have essentially vanished from the market, leaving MBS dependent on total-return focused investors who are increasingly finding better risk-adjusted opportunities in corporate credit and structured products.

Credit concerns are simultaneously emerging in the non-agency MBS space, where securities backed by non-conforming mortgages are experiencing payment delinquencies at rates not seen since 2020. Non-QM residential mortgage-backed securities issued in 2022 and 2023 are showing 60-day delinquency rates approaching 4.8%, nearly double the levels observed twelve months ago, according to analytics from Garrison Structured Finance. Rising unemployment in interest-rate-sensitive sectors including construction, real estate services, and mortgage origination is creating stress among borrowers with adjustable-rate products and those who purchased properties near market peaks. Rating agencies have placed 23 non-agency MBS tranches on negative watch in the past month, signaling potential downgrades that would trigger forced selling by investment-grade-only mandates.

Hedge funds and relative-value traders who had positioned for MBS spread tightening have unwound positions at significant losses, contributing to the selling cascade. Basis trades that profited from the relationship between MBS, interest rate swaps, and Treasury futures have experienced their worst performance since the March 2020 liquidity crisis, with several quantitative funds posting monthly losses exceeding 15% in their rates strategies. The dramatic repricing has raised concerns about potential forced liquidations if volatility continues to increase, which could create reflexive selling pressure similar to episodes witnessed during previous MBS market dislocations.

Looking ahead, strategists are advising clients to reduce MBS exposure and seek opportunities in more stable sectors of the fixed-income universe. The combination of negative convexity, persistent Fed selling, diminished bank demand, and emerging credit deterioration presents a particularly challenging environment for mortgage debt. Until inflation moderates convincingly and the Fed signals a genuine pivot toward accommodation—neither of which appears imminent—market participants expect continued underperformance from MBS relative to duration-matched Treasuries and investment-grade corporate bonds.

Key developments

  • Agency MBS spreads widened 12 basis points this week to 148 bps over Treasuries, with the Bloomberg MBS Index declining 1.8% in its worst weekly performance since July 2023
  • Prepayment speeds accelerated sharply to 18.2% CPR in February from 14.1% in January, creating negative convexity headwinds for higher-coupon securities
  • Commercial bank holdings of MBS have declined $127 billion over twelve months as traditional demand sources withdraw from the market amid Fed quantitative tightening
  • Non-agency MBS 60-day delinquency rates reached 4.8% on recent vintages, nearly double year-ago levels, with 23 tranches placed on negative ratings watch
  • Federal Reserve continues allowing $35 billion monthly in MBS runoff without reinvestment while officials signal extended higher rate environment

"The MBS market is facing a perfect storm of technical and fundamental headwinds that we haven't witnessed since the taper tantrum. Between Fed runoff, bank retrenchment, prepayment volatility, and now emerging credit stress in non-agency space, we're advising clients to materially reduce duration and mortgage exposure until we see stabilization in at least two of these four factors."

Jennifer Hastings, Head of Fixed Income Strategy at Wellington Capital Partners
